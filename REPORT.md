# Evidence-Grounded Research Report

## Glycemic Response to Glucose-Lowering Medications in Adults with Type 2 Diabetes: Patient Characteristics and Treatment Effectiveness Across Subgroups

---

## 1. Research Question

Among adults with Type 2 diabetes, which patient characteristics are associated with better glycemic response to different glucose-lowering medication classes, and what does existing clinical evidence suggest about treatment effectiveness across patient subgroups?

---

## 2. Background

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a heterogeneous condition managed with a growing array of glucose-lowering medication classes, including metformin, sulfonylureas (SU), sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA), dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i), thiazolidinediones (TZD), and insulin. These agents differ substantially in their mechanisms of action, glycemic efficacy, side-effect profiles, and non-glycemic benefits.

A central challenge in T2D management is that glycemic response to any given medication class varies considerably across individuals. Patient characteristics such as baseline HbA1c, body mass index (BMI), renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate, eGFR), age, and diabetes duration may modify treatment response. Understanding which patient characteristics predict better glycemic outcomes with specific drug classes is essential for precision medicine approaches to T2D management.

Clinical guidelines, including those from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), have increasingly emphasized that medication selection should account not only for glycemic efficacy but also for cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic benefits — particularly for SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_32_chunk_4; nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_81_chunk_4].

---

## 3. Research Approach

This report synthesizes evidence from two complementary sources:

1. **Clinical guideline literature**: Validated claims extracted from the NICE Type 2 Diabetes Management guideline (NG28, 2026 update) and the KDIGO Diabetes and CKD guideline, assessed for relevance, population match, intervention match, outcome match, and evidence strength.

2. **Patient cohort analysis**: Observational data from a cohort of 500 adults with T2D, including descriptive analyses of HbA1c change by medication class and multivariable linear regression examining patient-level predictors of glycemic response.

Evidence claims were graded for confidence (0–1 scale) based on study design quality, population relevance, outcome relevance, internal consistency, and evidence strength. Causal inference is not claimed from observational data. Associations are reported as associations only.

---

## 4. Literature Findings

### 4.1 Glycemic Efficacy and Weight Effects

The NICE Type 2 Diabetes Management guideline (NG28, 2026) states that **SGLT-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and tirzepatide reduce HbA1c and lead to weight loss as a beneficial side effect** in adults with T2D [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_52_chunk_5; confidence: 0.62]. This finding is drawn from a clinical evidence synthesis underpinning the guideline and is consistent across multiple guideline sections.

Additionally, the guideline notes that **weight reduction may be a side effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists, tirzepatide, SGLT-2 inhibitors, and metformin**, and that this may be an important consideration for patients and clinicians when selecting glucose-lowering medications [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_59_chunk_5; confidence: 0.55]. The hedged language ("may be") in the source text reflects appropriate uncertainty, particularly regarding metformin, whose weight-reduction effect is generally more modest than that of GLP-1 RAs or SGLT-2 inhibitors in the broader literature.

### 4.2 Cardiovascular and Renal Benefits

The NICE guideline explicitly states that **SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists are recommended as much for their cardiovascular and renal benefits as for their glycaemic benefits** [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_32_chunk_4; nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_81_chunk_4; confidence: 0.65 each]. This recommendation appears in at least two independent sections of the guideline, reinforcing its centrality to treatment decision-making.

The guideline further specifies that both SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists **reduce the risk of cardiovascular events** (including cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, and hospitalisation for heart failure) **and of developing end-stage renal disease** [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_52_chunk_5].

The NICE committee acknowledged that **people with T2D have an inherent increased lifetime risk of cardiovascular disease**, even before accounting for additional cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension and dyslipidaemia [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_36_chunk_7]. This context supports the broad recommendation for SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across the T2D population.

### 4.3 Subgroup-Level Evidence: A Critical Gap

**A consistent and important limitation across all validated literature claims is the absence of subgroup-level data identifying which patient characteristics predict better glycemic response to specific medication classes.** None of the validated evidence chunks from the NICE guideline provide comparative effectiveness data stratified by baseline HbA1c, BMI, age, eGFR, diabetes duration, or sex [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_52_chunk_5; nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_32_chunk_4; nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_59_chunk_5].

The NICE guideline does note that the evidence base on antidiabetic therapies covered a **diverse patient group**, including people with multiple cardiovascular risk factors, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and those with lower cardiovascular risk [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_34_chunk_2]. However, this observation describes the composition of the evidence base rather than providing subgroup-specific treatment effect estimates.

The guideline also references a specific subgroup — **adults with early onset T2D** — for whom further medication considerations apply [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_91_chunk_9], and notes that younger adults with T2D may share characteristics with this group for whom earlier intensive treatment is recommended [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_36_chunk_11]. However, no quantitative subgroup treatment effect data are provided in the retrieved evidence.

### 4.4 Medication Selection Principles

The NICE guideline advises clinicians to take into account the **effect of each medicine on HbA1c and weight**, as well as the **effectiveness of each medicine at preventing and managing cardiovascular disease**, when selecting treatment [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_22_chunk_7]. This reflects a multi-dimensional approach to medication selection that goes beyond glycemic efficacy alone.

The 2026 update to the NICE guideline includes new and updated recommendations on metformin, SGLT-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, and pioglitazone [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_129_chunk_2], though the specific content of these updates is not detailed in the retrieved evidence chunks.

### 4.5 Renal Considerations

The KDIGO Diabetes and CKD guideline identifies GLP-1 RAs (including albiglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide) as interventions of interest in adults and children with CKD and diabetes, with critical outcomes including kidney failure and all-cause hospitalisation [evidence_id: kdigo_diabetes_ckd_page_162_chunk_9]. This suggests that renal function (eGFR) is a clinically relevant patient characteristic in medication selection, though the retrieved evidence does not provide quantitative subgroup treatment effect estimates stratified by eGFR category.

## 6. Patient Cohort Findings

### 6.1 Cohort Overview

The patient cohort comprised **500 adults with T2D** with complete data on age, BMI, baseline HbA1c, follow-up HbA1c, eGFR, diabetes duration, and medication class. No missing values were present for any of these variables. Ten medication groups were represented.

### 6.2 Medication Group Distribution

| Medication Class | N | % of Cohort |
|---|---|---|
| Metformin + SGLT-2i | 82 | 16.4% |
| Metformin | 79 | 15.8% |
| SU + Metformin | 68 | 13.6% |
| Metformin + DPP-4i | 61 | 12.2% |
| SGLT-2i (monotherapy) | 45 | 9.0% |
| Metformin + GLP-1 RA | 40 | 8.0% |
| GLP-1 RA (monotherapy) | 37 | 7.4% |
| TZD + Metformin | 34 | 6.8% |
| Insulin + Metformin | 31 | 6.2% |
| Insulin (monotherapy) | 23 | 4.6% |

The most common regimens were metformin-based combinations. GLP-1 RA and SGLT-2i monotherapy groups were smaller (n = 37 and n = 45, respectively), which limits the precision of subgroup estimates for these groups.

### 6.3 HbA1c Change by Medication Group

Mean HbA1c change from baseline to follow-up by medication group is summarised below. **These are unadjusted, descriptive estimates from observational data. Differences between groups may reflect confounding by indication, differences in baseline HbA1c, and other patient characteristics rather than true causal treatment effects.**

| Medication Class | N | Mean Baseline HbA1c (%) | Mean Follow-up HbA1c (%) | Mean HbA1c Change (%) | SD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Insulin (monotherapy) | 23 | 7.94 | 6.36 | **−1.58** | 0.68 |
| Insulin + Metformin | 31 | 7.92 | 6.50 | −1.42 | 0.89 |
| GLP-1 RA (monotherapy) | 37 | 7.54 | 6.28 | −1.26 | 0.56 |
| Metformin + SGLT-2i | 82 | 7.41 | 6.17 | −1.25 | 0.53 |
| Metformin + GLP-1 RA | 40 | 7.58 | 6.44 | −1.14 | 0.65 |
| SU + Metformin | 68 | 7.36 | 6.26 | −1.10 | 0.55 |
| Metformin + DPP-4i | 61 | 7.51 | 6.45 | −1.06 | 0.42 |
| TZD + Metformin | 34 | 7.46 | 6.42 | −1.04 | 0.54 |
| SGLT-2i (monotherapy) | 45 | 7.63 | 6.68 | −0.95 | 0.41 |
| Metformin (monotherapy) | 79 | 7.24 | 6.48 | **−0.76** | 0.50 |

**Key observations (descriptive, unadjusted):**

- Insulin-based regimens were associated with the largest mean HbA1c reductions (−1.58% for monotherapy; −1.42% for insulin + metformin), but these groups also had the highest baseline HbA1c values (7.94% and 7.92%, respectively), which is a well-recognised source of confounding — higher baseline HbA1c is associated with greater absolute reduction regardless of treatment.
- Metformin + SGLT-2i was associated with a mean HbA1c reduction of −1.25%, the largest among non-insulin combination regimens, with a relatively lower baseline HbA1c (7.41%).
- GLP-1 RA monotherapy was associated with a mean reduction of −1.26%, with a baseline HbA1c of 7.54%.
- Metformin monotherapy was associated with the smallest mean reduction (−0.76%), consistent with its lower baseline HbA1c (7.24%) and its role as a first-line agent in patients with less severe hyperglycaemia.
- SGLT-2i monotherapy showed a smaller reduction (−0.95%) than Metformin + SGLT-2i (−1.25%), which may reflect differences in patient selection, baseline HbA1c, or the additive effect of metformin.

**These descriptive differences must be interpreted with caution.** Patients were not randomly assigned to treatment groups. Confounding by indication — whereby sicker or more hyperglycaemic patients are more likely to receive intensified therapy — is a major threat to validity in this observational dataset.

## 7. Statistical Findings

### 7.1 Multivariable Linear Regression: Predictors of HbA1c Change

A multivariable linear regression model was fitted with HbA1c change from baseline as the outcome and the following patient characteristics as predictors: age, BMI, baseline HbA1c, eGFR, and diabetes duration (n = 500).

| Predictor | Coefficient | Direction of Association |
|---|---|---|
| Age | −0.0014 | Older age → slightly less HbA1c reduction |
| BMI | +0.0065 | Higher BMI → slightly more HbA1c reduction |
| Baseline HbA1c | −0.1300 | Higher baseline HbA1c → more HbA1c reduction |
| eGFR | −0.0015 | Higher eGFR → slightly less HbA1c reduction |
| Diabetes duration | +0.0029 | Longer duration → slightly more HbA1c reduction |
| Intercept | −0.189 | — |

**R² = 0.023 (2.3%)**

### 7.2 Interpretation of Regression Results

**The overall model explains only 2.3% of the variance in HbA1c change**, indicating that the five patient characteristics included in this model — age, BMI, baseline HbA1c, eGFR, and diabetes duration — collectively have very limited predictive power for glycemic response in this cohort when medication class is not included as a predictor.

The strongest individual predictor is **baseline HbA1c** (coefficient: −0.130), indicating that patients with higher baseline HbA1c are associated with greater absolute HbA1c reduction. This is a well-recognised statistical phenomenon (regression to the mean) and does not necessarily reflect a causal relationship between baseline severity and treatment responsiveness.

The coefficients for age, BMI, eGFR, and diabetes duration are all very small in magnitude, suggesting that these variables, individually, have minimal association with HbA1c change in this dataset after accounting for baseline HbA1c. However, the low R² and the absence of medication class as a covariate in this model limit the interpretability of these coefficients.

**Important caveats:**
- This regression model does not include medication class as a predictor or as an interaction term, so it cannot address the primary research question of whether patient characteristics *differentially* predict response across drug classes.
- The model does not adjust for confounding by indication.
- No propensity score methods, interaction terms, or machine learning approaches were applied in the reported analysis, though these were pre-specified as planned analytical tasks.
- The low R² suggests that important predictors of glycemic response — including medication class, adherence, comorbidities, and unmeasured patient factors — are not captured in this model.

## 8. Comparison Between Patient Data and Literature

### 8.1 Areas of Consistency

The patient cohort findings are broadly consistent with the directional claims from the NICE guideline literature:

- **GLP-1 RA and SGLT-2i combinations with metformin were associated with meaningful HbA1c reductions** in the cohort (−1.26% and −1.25%, respectively), consistent with the NICE guideline's statement that these agents reduce HbA1c [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_52_chunk_5].
- **Metformin monotherapy was associated with the smallest HbA1c reduction** (−0.76%), consistent with its role as a first-line agent in patients with lower baseline HbA1c and less severe hyperglycaemia.
- The cohort data do not contradict the guideline's emphasis on the multi-benefit profile of SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RA, though the cohort analysis only captures glycemic outcomes and does not assess cardiovascular or renal endpoints.

### 8.2 Areas of Divergence or Uncertainty

- **Insulin-based regimens showed the largest unadjusted HbA1c reductions** in the cohort (−1.58% for insulin monotherapy), which may appear to conflict with guideline emphasis on SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RA. However, this likely reflects confounding by indication: insulin is typically prescribed to patients with higher baseline HbA1c and more advanced disease, as confirmed by the cohort data (mean baseline HbA1c 7.94% for insulin monotherapy vs. 7.24% for metformin monotherapy). The guideline's recommendations are not based solely on HbA1c-lowering magnitude but also on cardiovascular, renal, and safety profiles.
- **The cohort regression model explains only 2.3% of variance in HbA1c change**, suggesting that the patient characteristics examined (age, BMI, baseline HbA1c, eGFR, diabetes duration) are insufficient to predict glycemic response in this dataset. This is consistent with the literature's acknowledged gap in subgroup-level predictive data.
- **No subgroup analyses by patient characteristics were reported** in the cohort analysis, meaning the primary research question — which patient characteristics are associated with better glycemic response to *specific* drug classes — cannot be answered from the available data.

## 9. Conflicting Evidence

### 9.1 Within the Literature

No genuine scientific contradictions were identified among the validated literature claims. All substantive claims are drawn from the same NICE Type 2 Diabetes Management guideline (NG28, 2026) and are mutually consistent. Claims 4 and 5 are identical statements from two different pages of the same guideline, reinforcing rather than contradicting each other [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_32_chunk_4; nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_81_chunk_4].

A minor additive difference exists between Claim 2 (weight reduction associated with SGLT-2i, GLP-1 RA, and tirzepatide) and Claim 6 (weight reduction associated with the same agents plus metformin). This reflects different contextual emphases within the guideline rather than a scientific contradiction [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_52_chunk_5; nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_59_chunk_5].

### 9.2 Between Patient Data and Literature

The most notable tension between the cohort data and the literature is the **apparent superiority of insulin-based regimens in unadjusted HbA1c reduction**. This is almost certainly attributable to confounding by indication rather than a true treatment effect advantage of insulin over SGLT-2i or GLP-1 RA. The guideline's recommendations for SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RA are grounded in randomised controlled trial evidence that accounts for this confounding, whereas the cohort analysis does not.

### 9.3 Evidence Sufficiency

The evidence retrieval process reached its retry limit, indicating that the available evidence base — while internally consistent — is **insufficient to fully address the primary research question** regarding patient characteristics associated with differential glycemic response across drug classes. This is explicitly acknowledged in the contradiction analysis.

## 10. Limitations

### 10.1 Literature Limitations

1. **All validated literature claims are sourced from a single guideline** (NICE NG28, 2026). No independent primary studies, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses with PMID citations were available in the validated evidence set, limiting the ability to assess the strength of the underlying evidence base.
2. **No subgroup-level treatment effect data** are provided in any validated claim. The research question's core focus — patient characteristics predicting differential glycemic response — is not addressed by the available literature evidence.
3. **Effect sizes are not quantified** in the validated claims. The magnitude of HbA1c reduction, weight loss, or cardiovascular/renal benefit associated with each drug class is not specified in the retrieved evidence.
4. **The ADA Standards of Care** — a primary international guideline for T2D management — was not reachable at the time of evidence retrieval and is therefore not represented in this report.
5. **Causal inference cannot be drawn** from guideline recommendations alone, as these synthesise observational and trial evidence without providing the primary data.

### 10.2 Patient Cohort Limitations

1. **Observational design**: Patients were not randomly assigned to treatment groups. Confounding by indication is a major threat to validity. Patients with more severe hyperglycaemia or more advanced disease are more likely to receive intensified therapy (e.g., insulin), inflating apparent HbA1c reductions in those groups.
2. **No propensity score adjustment or IPTW** was applied in the reported analysis, despite being pre-specified as a planned analytical task. Unadjusted comparisons between medication groups should not be interpreted as causal treatment effects.
3. **No interaction analyses** between medication class and patient characteristics were reported, meaning the primary research question cannot be answered from the cohort data as analysed.
4. **Low model R² (2.3%)**: The regression model explains very little variance in HbA1c change, suggesting important predictors are unmeasured or unmodelled.
5. **Small group sizes**: Several medication groups have small sample sizes (e.g., insulin monotherapy n = 23, TZD + metformin n = 34), limiting statistical precision.
6. **Single follow-up time point**: The analysis uses a single follow-up HbA1c measurement. The timing of follow-up relative to treatment initiation is not specified, which may introduce variability.
7. **No adherence data**: Medication adherence (e.g., proportion of days covered) was not reported, which is a major determinant of glycemic response.
8. **No cardiovascular, renal, or weight outcomes** are reported in the cohort analysis, limiting comparison with the guideline's emphasis on multi-benefit drug selection.

---

## 11. Research Gaps

The following research gaps are identified based on the available evidence and the pre-specified analytical framework:

1. **Subgroup-level treatment effect data**: No validated evidence — from either the literature or the cohort analysis — provides quantitative estimates of differential glycemic response to specific drug classes stratified by patient characteristics (baseline HbA1c, BMI, age, eGFR, diabetes duration, sex, or presence of CVD/HF/CKD). This is the most critical gap relative to the research question.

2. **Interaction analyses**: The cohort regression model does not include drug class × patient characteristic interaction terms. Pre-specified subgroup analyses (e.g., by baseline HbA1c category, BMI category, eGFR category) were not reported. These analyses are necessary to address the research question.

3. **Confounding adjustment**: Propensity score methods (IPTW or matching) were pre-specified but not applied. Without these, observational comparisons between medication groups are not interpretable as treatment effects.

4. **Network meta-analysis**: A Bayesian random-effects network meta-analysis was pre-specified to simultaneously compare HbA1c-lowering efficacy across drug classes within patient subgroups. This analysis was not conducted or reported.

5. **Machine learning approaches**: LASSO regression, gradient boosting, and causal forest methods were pre-specified for identifying non-linear and interaction effects among patient characteristics. These were not applied.

6. **Non-glycemic outcomes in the cohort**: The cohort analysis does not capture weight change, cardiovascular events, or renal outcomes, limiting comparison with the guideline's multi-benefit framework for SGLT-2i and GLP-1 RA.

7. **Adherence and persistence data**: Medication adherence was not measured or adjusted for, which is a major determinant of real-world glycemic response.

8. **Broader literature synthesis**: The validated evidence base is limited to a single guideline. Systematic reviews and RCTs with PMID-cited primary data — particularly those examining treatment effect heterogeneity by patient subgroup — are needed to address the research question.

9. **Early-onset T2D subgroup**: The NICE guideline references adults with early-onset T2D as a distinct subgroup for whom specific medication considerations apply [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_91_chunk_9], but no quantitative treatment effect data for this subgroup are available in the retrieved evidence.

## 12. Conclusion

This report synthesises evidence from the NICE Type 2 Diabetes Management guideline (NG28, 2026) and an observational cohort of 500 adults with T2D to address the question of which patient characteristics are associated with better glycemic response to different glucose-lowering medication classes.

**From the literature**, the NICE guideline provides moderate-confidence evidence that SGLT-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and tirzepatide reduce HbA1c and lead to weight loss in adults with T2D [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_52_chunk_5; confidence: 0.62], and that SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists are recommended as much for their cardiovascular and renal benefits as for their glycaemic benefits [evidence_id: nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_32_chunk_4; nice_type2_diabetes_management_page_81_chunk_4; confidence: 0.65]. However, **no validated evidence from the available literature identifies specific patient characteristics that predict better glycemic response to particular drug classes**. This represents a consistent and critical gap across all retrieved evidence.

**From the patient cohort**, unadjusted descriptive analyses suggest that insulin-based regimens, GLP-1 RA, and metformin + SGLT-2i combinations were associated with the largest mean HbA1c reductions, while metformin monotherapy was associated with the smallest. However, these differences are substantially confounded by differences in baseline HbA1c and disease severity across treatment groups, and **causal inference is not warranted from these observational data**. A multivariable regression model including age, BMI, baseline HbA1c, eGFR, and diabetes duration explained only 2.3% of variance in HbA1c change, indicating that these characteristics alone are poor predictors of glycemic response in this dataset.

**The primary research question — which patient characteristics are associated with better glycemic response to specific drug classes — cannot be answered from the available evidence.** Addressing this question requires pre-specified subgroup analyses with interaction testing, propensity score adjustment for confounding, and ideally a network meta-analysis or causal forest approach applied to adequately powered datasets. These analyses were pre-specified but not yet conducted. Future work should prioritise these methods, alongside integration of primary RCT data with PMID-cited subgroup analyses, to advance precision medicine approaches to T2D pharmacotherapy.
